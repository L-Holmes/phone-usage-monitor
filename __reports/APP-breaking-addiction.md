


Acceptance & values tools (ACT) — strongest evidence by far. In the RCT your report cites this hit a 93% reduction in use and 54% complete cessation. Features: urge-surfing logs (notice the urge, rate it, watch it pass without acting), cognitive-defusion exercises ("I'm having the thought that I want to…" rather than obeying it), and a values board where they define what they're choosing toward (relationship, focus, self-respect). This is where your "delay the response" idea actually belongs — the pause is valuable not as dopamine-denial but as the gap where an urge-surf or values check happens.
CBT trigger + restructuring tools. Daily check-ins that map trigger → feeling → thought → behaviour (loneliness, boredom, stress are the big ones), plus a relapse chain-analyser they fill in after a slip, and worksheets that challenge permissive thoughts ("just five minutes won't matter"). High effect sizes in the same meta-analysis.
Contingency management (reward the right thing). Strong operant-conditioning evidence (up to 18x abstinence in trials). Critical detail from your report: reward engagement with recovery behaviours (completing a module, logging a mood, doing the coping task) — not self-reported abstinence, which is unverifiable and invites lying. Gamified points, unlockables, even small real rewards.
Motivational interviewing, especially first 30 days. This is your retention play — most users drop off fast. A conversational agent that helps them articulate their own reasons for change and surfaces the gap between current behaviour and their goals. Reduces early dropout, which is where apps die.
Stimulus control + habit replacement + decoupling. Moderate effect (d ≈ 0.52). This is where your "change location / do other things" and "fake cigarette" ideas land. Features: device blockers, time-locks, device-free zones, and an SOS "panic button" that locks the screen and launches a competing physical action (paced breathing, intense sensory input). On your fake-search idea specifically — there's a real mechanism there (cue exposure + response prevention, and true decoupling: mimic the motor sequence but abort before payoff), but a naive "search returns nothing" gimmick risks backfiring: it still exposes them to the cue, may just push them to a real site, and can feel patronising. The evidence-based version pairs the cue with a coping response, ideally with rationale, not a bait-and-switch.
Pharmacotherapy — lowest, and not really an app feature. Weak evidence, off-label only. Best you do is route severe/treatment-refractory users to offline clinical help.

Two things that will make or break this regardless of features:

Kill the rigid streak counter. This is the biggest design trap. A "30 days → one slip → back to 0" model triggers the Abstinence Violation Effect: the reset itself produces the shame that drives a small lapse into a full relapse ("I've already broken it, so what's the point"). Use a rolling consistency percentage (a 5-min slip = 96.7% over 30 days, not zero), a "lapse day" buffer, and award points for logging a slip honestly. Reward transparency, not perfection.

Screen for moral incongruence at onboarding. Some users aren't behaviourally dysregulated at all — their distress comes from conflict with their values/religion (often lower actual usage but high shame). If you push restriction protocols at them you make the shame worse. Route those users to acceptance/defusion content instead of blockers. A short PPCS + moral-disapproval screener at signup decides which track they get.

On your "replace the dopamine / get them off the phone" question — it's a bit of both, but the framing to drop is the dopamine-detox one. The goal isn't a substitute hit; it's psychological flexibility plus competing actions plus removing cues. And yes, watch that the app itself doesn't become the compulsive object — discreet icon, non-triggering notifications, and don't engineer it to be sticky in the same way the thing you're treating is.
